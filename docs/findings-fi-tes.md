## Reading these numbers

Written by hand. Everything above this line is generated by `bun run report`.

### Reranking works, and it is strictly additive here

Hybrid retrieval alone puts the right paragraph first for 31.9% of queries.
Every reranker roughly doubles that. More importantly, `bun run examples` reports
**zero regressions**: across the 72 scorable queries, no reranker demoted a gold
passage that the first stage had already placed first. There was no accuracy to
trade away, so the fusion strategy this benchmark was going to test next — RRF
over the first-stage rank and the reranker score — has nothing to fix and was
not built.

### Jev does not beat a dedicated cross-encoder

Voyage `rerank-2.5` is ahead of the best Jev configuration by 2.7 points of R@1
at section level, and costs slightly less per thousand queries. That is the
honest headline and it should not be buried: if the only requirement is "rank
this shortlist well", a purpose-built reranker is still the thing to reach for.

### Jev beats a general LLM asked the identical question, by a lot

`hybrid+llm` runs `gpt-4.1-mini` on the *same* instructions and criteria —
imported from `rerank/jev.ts`, not rewritten — and reads P(yes) from logprobs.
It reaches 41.7% against Jev's 55.6%, at eight times the cost and five times the
latency. The comparison that matters for a team choosing an architecture is not
Jev against a reranker; it is Jev against the LLM call they would otherwise
write.

### The probabilities are calibrated; the LLM's are not

The abstention curve is the clearest result in the run:

| top-1 score threshold | `jev-batched` answered / precision | `llm` answered / precision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0 | 100% / 56% | 100% / 42% |
| 0.8 | 69% / 64% | 99% / 42% |
| 0.9 | 25% / 89% | 99% / 42% |

`gpt-4.1-mini` is flat. Its logprobs saturate at 0.000 and 1.000, so thresholding
them discards nothing and tells you nothing — the model cannot report when it is
wrong. A Noul can: at 0.9, a quarter of the queries can be answered
automatically at 89% top-1 precision and the rest escalated. For a system that
has to be right rather than fast, that gate is worth more than the 2.7 points
Voyage is ahead by, because Voyage's own curve is much shallower (58% → 62%).

### Batching is free quality

One request per 15 candidates instead of one per candidate: 168 requests instead
of 2520, 334 ms instead of 892 ms at p50, \$0.90 instead of \$1.21 per thousand
queries — and *better* results, 55.6% against 52.8%.

The TypeSafe rerank cookbook measures 12x on cost for the shape it uses (one
document, many questions). This is the other shape — many passages, one question
each — where the passages dominate the tokens and cannot be amortised, so the
saving is 26% on tokens. The 2.7x on latency is the real prize, and it comes
from collapsing 30 round trips into 2.

### The RRF hybrid is not earning its place *here*

`vector` alone reaches 41.7% R@1 at section level. Fusing it with Postgres FTS
gives `hybrid` 31.9% — *worse*. The lexical side scores 4.2%, and RRF has no way
to know it should be ignored; it promotes a bad list's top results on equal
terms with a good list's. After reranking the two are level (52.8% vs 51.4%),
and `vector`'s shortlist ceiling is the higher of the two (72.2% vs 70.8%).

The companion run on MuPLeR contradicts this, and the contradiction is the
useful part: there `hybrid` scores 73.0% against `vector`'s 65.5%, because
MuPLeR's lexical side is genuinely strong (33.5% R@1, not 4.2%). Fusion helps
exactly when both lists are worth fusing.

So the claim is not "hybrid search is bad". It is that **RRF has no opinion about
whether a list deserves to be in the fusion**, and a weak lexical side is not
free — it costs ten points here. Measure the lexical arm on its own before
fusing it, which is one query and no model.

Note the scope: Postgres `ts_rank_cd` is not BM25, and a real BM25
implementation would very likely change the private corpus number.

### The question matters more than the model

The relevance judgment used here demands provenance: the passage has to come
from the document the question names. Swapping it for the portable judgment
written for MuPLeR, with everything else identical:

| strategy | corpus-specific question | portable question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `hybrid+jev-noul` | 52.8% | 48.6% |
| `hybrid+jev-batched` | 55.6% | 51.4% |
| `hybrid+jev-score` | 55.6% | 43.1% |

And on MuPLeR the same swap runs the other way, 77.0% against 97.0%. Each corpus
is served best by the judgment written for what actually separates a right
answer there — and using the other one costs between four and twenty points.

`jev-score` is the most sensitive of the three. A rubric whose levels describe
distinctions the corpus does not have is worse than a binary criterion that at
least asks one thing.

This ablation also settles a question about the Voyage result above: the
corpus-specific judgment is already the better of the two tried, so Voyage's 2.7
point lead is not an artefact of a badly worded question.

### A deeper shortlist moves recall, not precision

Re-running at `--candidates=100`, section level:

| strategy | R@1 k=30 → k=100 | R@10 k=30 → k=100 | \$/1000 queries |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `hybrid` | 31.9% → 33.3% | 58.3% → 58.3% | — |
| `hybrid+jev-batched` | 56.9% → 58.3% | 65.3% → 72.2% | \$0.90 → \$2.95 |
| `hybrid+voyage` | 58.3% → 61.1% | 69.4% → 76.4% | \$0.74 → \$2.39 |

The shortlist ceiling rises from 70.8% to 84.7% — nearly fourteen points more of
the answer set becomes reachable — and R@1 moves by one to three. R@10 is where
the gain lands, about seven points, for 3.3x the cost.

The interesting part is the change of bottleneck. At k=30 `jev-batched` reached
65.3% R@10 against a 70.8% ceiling: it had captured 92% of what it was given,
and the first stage was the limit. At k=100 it reaches 72.2% against 84.7%, or
85%. **Depth hands the reranker more than it can use.** Whatever the next gain
is, it is not more candidates.

### Fusing rankers does not help, because they fail together

Every strategy stores its per-candidate scores, so `bun run fuse` searches 245
combinations — RRF, CombSUM, CombMNZ, Borda, geometric mean, max, and weighted
variants — for no API cost, selecting on one half of the queries and reporting
the other.

| corpus | best single (test) | best fusion (test) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the private corpus, 36 test queries | 58.3% (`voyage`) | 58.3% |
| MuPLeR, 100 test queries | 95.0% (`voyage`) | 95.0% |

Nothing beats the best single ranker on held-out queries on either corpus. The
reason is measurable: the rerankers make the *same* mistakes.

| | failures | shared with `jev-batched` | Jaccard |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `hybrid+jev-batched` | 31 of 72 | — | — |
| `hybrid+voyage` | 30 | 27 | 0.79 |
| `hybrid+jev-score` | 34 | 27 | 0.71 |
| `hybrid+llm-4.1-mini` | 43 | 30 | 0.68 |

27 queries are ranked wrong by every reranker tested. An *oracle* free to pick
the best reranker per query would fix 20 of 72 — and on MuPLeR, 15 of 200.
Fusion works when rankers are wrong in different places; these are wrong in the
same places, and mostly because the answer was never retrieved.

### Open weights do not substitute here

[Laya](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya) (Apache-2.0) answers the same
three primitives as Jev from local weights, and was asked the identical
question via `bun run dump-question`. On the same shortlists it scores **8.3%
R@1 — well below the 31.9% of not reranking at all.** Its scores bunch at the
top of the range (0.96, 0.96, 0.93, 0.87 on a shortlist whose first candidate
is wrong), which is the "top-of-scale cases look alike" failure, and it cannot
be fixed by rewording without breaking the comparison.

On this machine it also cost 768 ms per decision on CPU; the project reports
39.5 ms on a T4, so the latency is not the model's fault. The accuracy is.

### What actually limits the result

The shortlist ceiling is 70.8%. For **21 of the 72 scorable queries the gold
passage never entered the top 30 at all**, and no reranker can retrieve what it
was not given. Jev reaches 69.4% R@10 against that 70.8% ceiling, which means it
has very nearly exhausted the shortlist it was handed.

So the next experiment is not a better reranker. It is a deeper shortlist —
and batching is what makes that affordable, since 100 candidates is 7 requests
per query rather than 100.
